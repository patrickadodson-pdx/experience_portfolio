Data & Analytics · Exposure & perspective

The data platform gets built. The value stalls.

I have spent a career moving through technical domains, from enterprise hardware and printing to semiconductor manufacturing, venture-backed startups, and the technical and policy governance of the internet. In every one the job underneath was the same: stand between the people who build the technology and understand how remarkable it is, and the people who have to adopt it and are mostly asking "so what." Translate the first into the second, and move the organization past both the builder's certainty and the entrenched skepticism that meets it.

Data and analytics is one more of those domains, and I come at it the way I have for years. Not as the deepest engineer in the room, but as the person who can sit with that engineer, pull the honest version of what the technology is for out of the conversation, and put it in front of someone wary of the usual promises in a way that holds up. I have built on these tools by hand enough to keep myself honest about what is real, and to learn where to poke when it sounds too good to be true. What follows is a map of the terrain and the gap I keep watching organizations fall into.

The pattern

Every data architecture era is basically trying to re-solve the same problem.

A business needs to record what happens reliably and analyze it flexibly, and those two jobs pull the architecture in opposite directions. Each era is a different treaty between them, and each transition follows the same shape: the new platform augments the incumbent, then becomes the system of record.

Figure 1 · The data architecture transition, 1990s to today
1990s to 2000s

The data warehouse

Analysis split off from operations. Data was copied out of the systems running the business into a separate, governed store built only for reporting, reshaped on the way in. Trustworthy and fast for structured reports, but rigid, expensive, and blind to anything that was not rows and columns.

2010s

The data lake

Cheap object storage inverted the model: store everything now in any format, decide what it means later. Scale and flexibility for machine learning arrived, but without structure many lakes became swamps, and most teams ended up running a lake and a warehouse side by side, reconciling two versions of the truth.

Late 2010s to 2020s

The lakehouse

A software layer over cheap storage gave the lake the reliability and governance of a warehouse: transactional integrity, enforced schema, and time travel. One copy of the data could finally serve both business intelligence and AI, retiring the two-system split.

2025 and beyond

The agentic data platform

The current transition. With the open table format settled, storage lock-in stopped being the deciding factor and the contest moved up to the catalog, governance, and the context an autonomous agent needs to query data safely. The platform is becoming less where data lives and more where governed agents do the work.

The recurring tension

Record versus analyze

Systems that reliably record what happens pull the architecture one way; systems that flexibly analyze it pull the other. Warehouse, lake, and lakehouse are each a different treaty between those two jobs. Naming which side a platform was built for predicts where it will strain.

The 2026 resolution

Open format, contested control

A shared open table format now spans every major engine, so keeping one copy of data and querying it from many engines is normal, and storage lock-in is largely dead as a strategic lever. The competition moved one layer up, to the catalog and governance plane that decides whether an architecture is interoperable, trustworthy, and ready for agents.

The terrain, described neutrally

The stack, by layer.

A modern lakehouse is not one product. It is a set of layers, and most confusion comes from expecting one layer to do a job another was built for. Read bottom to top, storage up to consumption. The platforms most people name sit across several layers at once. I describe this as a map, not a recommendation.

Where the platforms sit

Databricks, Snowflake, and Microsoft Fabric bundle the middle and upper layers into one managed platform, on the bet that unified governance beats stitching best-of-breed tools together. As a rough guide, Databricks leans toward teams that build and train models; Snowflake toward teams that consume and analyze.

Why the catalog is the decision

With the table format adopted across every major engine, the format no longer creates lock-in. The catalog and governance layer is where interoperability, access control, and agent-ready context are won or lost, which is why the platform choice increasingly hinges on L4, not L1.

Where the industry missteps

The data-to-insights gap.

Everything above is real engineering, and most of it is done well. The problem is not the stack. The problem is what happens after it is stood up, if what comes next is not anticipated and planned for.

The dominant pattern I watch is engineering-led: build the platform, migrate the data, install the agentic tooling, and then go looking for the business problem it was supposed to solve. It is the same failure mode I have watched for twenty years in every other technology wave, and it produces the same result. A capable platform, a proud data team, but end users or buyers not understanding what exactly to do with it, and a CFO who cannot find the return on the investment.

The industry solved the engineering and skipped the adoption and value translation. That is why the business cannot see the value yet. The data got governed. The work never changed.

A platform does not create value. A decision made differently because of it does, and the distance between those two is human, not architectural. It is the analyst still running their old spreadsheet because no one showed them why the governed table is worth trusting, and the executive who funded the whole migration and cannot name one business outcome resulting from the investment. Closing that gap is its own discipline, and it is the one the industry keeps treating as someone else's job.

That discipline is my ground. I do not build the data platforms, but I have worked close enough to them to assess the terrain quickly, ask the questions that open up insights and possibilities, then help map a path through to the other side so organizations can improve and change how they work. The correction is not more platform. It is to start from the outcome a business leader would recognize and work back to where in the stack, and the organization, the obstacles exist.

What this changes about the question

The useful question is almost never "which platform." It is "which specific outcome does finance need to see, and which single step on the maturity ladder below is actually blocking it." Answer that, and most of the platform debate resolves itself.

Where you are

The data maturity ladder.

Most organizations sit at an earlier stage than they think, and the value of the stack above is capped by the rung they are actually on. Each rung is defined by what the organization can actually do with its data, not by which tools are installed. This is the frame I would use to place a team and name the real obstacle before recommending anything, because prescription before diagnosis is malpractice.

Figure 3 · Data & analytics maturity, five rungs, frontier framed as adopted value
1
Reactive

Reporting is manual and after the fact. Spreadsheets, one-off exports, and numbers that do not reconcile between teams.

To advance: a single governed source of truth, so people stop arguing about whose number is right.
2
Foundational

A warehouse or BI layer exists. Dashboards are trusted for structured reporting, but the data is siloed and slow to change.

To advance: cheap open storage and a table format, so unstructured data and machine learning join the same foundation.
3
Integrated

A governed lakehouse serves BI and data science from one copy of the data. Access, lineage, and quality are managed centrally.

To advance: models and agents running in production against that governed data, not just explored in notebooks.
4
Predictive

Machine learning and agents run in production and feed decisions. Features, models, and monitoring are managed as first-class assets.

To advance: the rung most teams skip, getting the organization to actually adopt what the platform makes possible, and proving the result to finance.
5
Adopted, and the value is visible to finance

The organization changed how it works because of the platform, the new way is the default rather than the exception, and the outcome shows up in a number a CFO recognizes. This is the rung that justifies everything below it.

Where the gap is: most enterprises sit between rungs two and three, most investment is aimed at rung four, and almost no one is deliberately building toward rung five.

Maturity ladders are a lens, not a verdict. A team can be advanced in one domain and reactive in another, and the useful question is rarely the overall rung but which specific step is blocking the outcome the business actually wants.
